I bear the name Pirrip, as attested by my father’s gravestone and the testimony of my sister, Mrs. Joe Gargery, who wed the blacksmith. Never having beheld my parents, nor any likeness thereof—since their days antedated the invention of photography—I formed my earliest notions of their appearance from the very stones that marked their resting place. The angular letters upon my father’s slab suggested to my youthful imagination a squat, corpulent, sable‑haired man, his locks curling like serpents. From the inscription ‘Also Georgiana Wife of the Above’ I inferred that my mother was flecked with freckles and of a delicate constitution. Moreover, the five modest stone lozenges, each about eighteen inches in length, aligned neatly beside their grave, commemorating five infant brothers who had relinquished the struggle for life almost at its inception, led me to cherish the pious belief that they had entered the world lying upon their backs, their tiny hands forever lodged within the pockets of their breeches, never to be withdrawn during their brief sojourn.

Conscience, when it accuses a man or a boy, is a dreadful thing; yet when, in the case of a lad, that secret weight partners with another secret burden concealed within the leg of his breeches, the punishment becomes, as I can attest, most grievous.
