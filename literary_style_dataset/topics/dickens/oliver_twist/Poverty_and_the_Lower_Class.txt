An admirable illustration of the sway wielded by raiment, young Oliver Twist was! Enveloped in the blanket that had hitherto shielded him, he might have been the scion of a noble line or a destitute wretch; it would have been no easy task for the most haughty stranger to assign him his true station. For eight or ten months Oliver endured a relentless course of imposture and oppression. He was nurtured by hand, the infant’s pitiful condition reported by the workhouse to the parish, which, with dignified inquiry, asked whether any woman of the house could furnish the nourishment he required. The workhouse answered in humility that none existed. Thereupon the parish, with magnanimous resolve, “farmed” the child, sending him three miles off to a branch‑workhouse where twenty or thirty juvenile offenders rolled about the floor, feasting on the scant allowance of a workhouse matron who received but a half‑penny per head each week. A half‑penny’s pittance formed a most generous diet for a child—enough to overfill his stomach and render him uncomfortable. The matron, versed in the ways of prudence, appropriated most of the stipend to her own use, consigning the rising generation to an even slimmer ration. Thus she descended to a still lower depth, revealing herself as a most adept experimental philosopher.

--- Paragraph 10 ---
The members of this board were sage, deep‑minded, philosophical men; and when they turned their attention to the workhouse they uncovered at once what ordinary minds could not grasp—the poor delighted in it! It was a veritable place of public amusement for the lower classes; a tavern where no charge was demanded; a public establishment offering breakfast, dinner, tea and supper throughout the year—a brick‑and‑mortar Elysium wherein all was play and no labour. “Ah!” said the board, assuming a knowing air; “we are the very persons to set this to rights; we shall put an end to it forthwith.” Accordingly they enacted a rule that every poor soul should have the alternative—Surely not to compel any—of being starved by a gradual process within the house, or by a swift one without it. To that end they contracted with the water‑works for an unlimited supply, with a corn‑factory for a periodic dribble of oatmeal, and issued three thin gruel meals a day, an onion twice a week, and half a roll on Sundays. They promulgated other wise and humane regulations concerning the ladies, which need not be recounted; they undertook to divorce poor married persons, in consequence of the expense of a suit in Doctors’ Commons, and, instead of obliging a man to support his family, they removed his family from him and made him a bachelor! It is hard to say how many applicants for relief, under these two heads, might have risen in all ranks of society, had they not been coupled with the workhouse; but the board, being far‑seeing, provided against this. Relief was inseparable from the workhouse and the gruel, and that very circumstance frightened the populace.

The hall in which the boys took their meals was a vast stone chamber, at one end of which stood a copper kettle. From this the master, clothed in an apron, and assisted by one or two women, ladled out the gruel at mealtimes. Each boy received a single porringer, and no more—except on great public occasions when he might obtain two ounces and a quarter of bread besides. The bowls never required washing; the boys polished them with their spoons till they shone, an operation which, owing to the size of the spoons, never consumed much time. Meanwhile they stared at the copper with eager eyes, as though they could have devoured the very bricks of which it was made, sucking their fingers most assiduously in order to snatch any stray splashes of gruel that might have spattered thereon. Boys generally possess hearty appetites; Oliver Twist and his companions suffered the tortures of slow starvation for three months. At last they grew so voracious that one tall boy, unused to such fare (his father having kept a small cook‑shop), hinted darkly that unless he received another basin of gruel each day he feared he might some night eat the boy who slept next to him, a weakly youth of tender age. He bore a wild, hungry eye, and the others believed him. A council was called; lots were cast to determine who should approach the master after supper and request more; the lot fell to Oliver Twist.
