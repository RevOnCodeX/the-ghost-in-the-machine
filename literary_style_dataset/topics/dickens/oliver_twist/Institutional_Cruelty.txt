At last, he exhaled, sneezed, and cried with a volume that eclipsed the wail of any infant thus newly come into the world.

For an octennium of ciceronian folly, Oliver was subjected to a regimen of perfidy and guile. Reared by the rod, his youthful resentment was duly reported by the administrators of the workhouse to the overseeing magistrates. These latter, addressing their superiors with austere inquiry, demanded whether a female of any standing resided within the house to nourish and solace the orphan. The reply was as evasive as the winds: no such woman existed. Whereupon, the authorities, with magnanimous benevolence, decreed that Oliver be “farmed”—that is, dispatched to an extramural institution wherein a cadre of juvenile offenders, numbering some twenty or thirty, were left to gnaw their fillings all day without the encumbrance of sustenance or raiment, under the tutelage of an elderly dame whose compensation per capita was a paltry sevenpence and a halfpenny weekly. Such a pittance, one might remark, could amply sate a child’s gut and more, yet this virago, ever a pragmatist of effrontery, pocketed the lion’s share, leaving her charges with even scantier rations than before.

Here the board, in their sagacity, had resolved that the paupers cherished their lot too soundly. For what utility was a forsake if not to endure mild suffering? Thus, they instituted a dual tyranny: starve within the workhouse, or starve forth—oh, the glories of their ingenuity! They contracted with plumbers and grocers to furnish oatmeal and water, and doled out thin pottage three times daily, with stale onions and communion rolls on Sundays. They also instituted divorce proceedings without recourse, and severed familial bonds with surgical precision. Yet their edict was clear: all relief demanded submission to the workhouse, and the thought thereof curdled the blood of all poorfolk.
