Behold a striking testament to the tyranny of tailor’s work, this young Oliver Twist! Swathed in the solitary blanket that had hitherto constituted his entire wardrobe, he might have passed for the scion of a duke or the offspring of a mendicant; the proudest aristocrat would have stumbled in assigning the infant his proper niche in the great hierarchy of mankind. But once invested in the antiquated calico vestments, yellowed by generations of parish service, he was branded and catalogued, sinking instantly into his ordained groove—a parish foundling, the workhouse orphan, the lowly, half-famished drudge—destined to be knocked about and battered through the world’s thoroughfares, scorned by the multitude, unwept by any.

The guardians of this establishment were profoundly sage, abysmally deep, and eminently philosophical gentlemen; and when they deigned to direct their intellects toward the workhouse, they instantly penetrated a mystery that would have baffled common understandings—the paupers actually *enjoyed* their residence! It served as a perpetual palace of diversion for the indigent; a tavern demanding no reckoning; a ceaseless round of breakfast, dinner, tea, and supper throughout the twelvemonth; a brick-and-mortar Elysium where labour was unknown and leisure absolute. “Ha!” cried the Board, with an air of profound penetration; “we are the very fellows to remedy this abuse; we shall put a stop to it forthwith.” Accordingly, they decreed that the destitute should possess the alternative—for they would compel no man, oh no—of perishing by inches within the walls, or by a swifter method without. With this benevolent aim, they negotiated with the water-works for an unbounded torrent; and with a corn-factor for periodic pittances of oatmeal; and ordained three daily meals of watery gruel, an onion bi-weekly, and half a roll on Sundays. They enacted a multitude of other wise and tender ordinances concerning the female sex, which decorum forbids rehearsing; they charitably undertook to sunder married couples, sparing them the ruinous expense of a suit in Doctors’ Commons; and, instead of enforcing a husband’s duty to maintain his family, as heretofore, they relieved him of his encumbrances and restored him to the blessings of celibacy! One shudders to think what crowds of claimants these two latter clauses might have summoned from every stratum of society, had they not been yoked to the workhouse and the gruel; but the Board were far-sighted men, and had forestalled the difficulty. Relief was made inseparable from the House and the porridge; and that prospect frightened the applicants away.

Let not the adversaries of “the system” imagine that, during the term of his solitary confinement, Oliver was deprived of the salutary benefits of exercise, the solace of society, or the comforts of spiritual consolation. Regarding exercise: the weather being sharp and frosty, he was permitted to perform his morning ablutions beneath the pump in a stone courtyard, under the superintendence of Mr. Bumble, who prevented a chill from settling on his frame by the frequent application of the cane, causing a most salutary tingling to diffuse itself through his extremities. Regarding society: he was conveyed every alternate day into the dining-hall where the boys assembled, and there sociably flagellated as a public spectacle and warning. And far from being denied religious privilege, he was kicked into the same apartment each evening at prayer-time, there to listen to, and derive comfort from, a general supplication of the boys, into which a special clause had been inserted by Board authority, wherein they petitioned to be rendered good, virtuous, contented, and obedient, and to be shielded from the sins and vices of Oliver Twist—whom the prayer explicitly designated as being under the exclusive patronage of the Powers of Darkness, and a direct manufacture of the Devil himself.

For the next eight or ten months, Oliver was the victim of a systematic course of treachery and imposition. He was brought up by hand. The famished and destitute condition of the infant orphan was duly reported by the workhouse authorities to the parish authorities. The parish authorities inquired with dignity of the workhouse authorities whether there was no female then resident in “the house” capable of imparting to Oliver Twist the consolation and nourishment of which he stood in need. The workhouse authorities replied with humility that there was not. Upon this, the parish authorities magnanimously and humanely resolved that Oliver should be “farmed”—or, in other words, dispatched to a satellite workhouse some three miles distant, where twenty or thirty other juvenile offenders against the poor-laws rolled about the floor all day, without the inconvenience of too much food or too much clothing, under the parental superintendence of an elderly female, who received the culprits at and for the consideration of sevenpence-halfpenny per small head per week. Sevenpence-halfpenny’s worth per week is a good round diet for a child; a great deal may be procured for sevenpence-halfpenny, quite enough to overload its stomach and make it uncomfortable. The elderly female was a woman of wisdom and experience; she knew what was good for children; and she had a very accurate perception of what was good for herself. So she appropriated the lion’s share of the weekly stipend to her own use, and consigned the rising parochial generation to even a shorter allowance than was originally provided. Thereby discovering in the lowest depth a deeper still; and proving herself a very great experimental philosopher.
