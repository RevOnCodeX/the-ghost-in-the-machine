The secret of her magnetic allure, which many men and some women—particularly Miss Austen herself—have perceptibly felt, lies less in articulable explanation than in the sensation of feeling it; like most charms it is experienced rather than defined. Elizabeth, for instance, belongs to the vivacious cohort of Venus’s worshippers. Austen herself was ever‑so reluctant to linger over physical description, and concerning the heroine one hears merely that her eyes are fine, that she sometimes enjoys a bright complexion, and that she is not tall. Yet the very distinction of Elizabeth among lively heroines rests chiefly upon two qualities: a keen mental activity, bordering on the admirable form of “strong‑mindedness”, and an absence of ill‑nature, despite a ready propensity for teasing and a tongue that is sharp enough to cut. She can match an antagonist in repartee and, though she never initiates attack nor “scratches”, she does not relent when provoked. A few of her early utterances, coloured by obsolete phrasing and manners, may hint at a slight pertness, but this is immaterial, and when she faces serious matters—most notably the climactic proposal from Darcy—she remains impeccable.
