The composition of this work, it may be of use to remark, first saw the light in the year 1796, when its author was scarce yet two-and-twenty. Though it underwent revision at Chawton some fifteen years hence, and was not published until 1813, yet the union of youth’s unbridled vigor and the seasoned judgment of maturity, as I conceive, doth impart to it a structure more harmonious than its fellows. The tale, though not intricately wrought, is yet so neatly constructed that to pluck a single thread would imperil the whole. The elopement of Lydia and Wickham, unlike the theatrical intrusions of other novels, doth arise naturally from the preceding narrative and bringeth the story to its fitting close. Each episode—Jane and Bingley’s tender attachment, the arrival of Mr. Collins, the sojourn at Hunsford, the Derbyshire tour—findeth its due place in this unobtrusive yet masterful design. There is no reliance on the arts of concealment or false mystery, which, though they may serve to enliven other tales, would seem out of place in a narrative so delicately balanced. Miss Austen, ever fond of misunderstandings, hath here contented herself with those naturally arising from Wickham’s misrepresentation of Darcy’s conduct and the gradual thawing of Elizabeth’s prejudice into affection. Should the dramatist attempt to translate this work to the stage, he might find its situations too refined for the footlights, its characters too nuanced for the rapt attention of the pit and gallery. Yet were he to try, he would not be beset by those flaws of construction which, when adapted to the stage, would betray their origin in the liberties of the written word.

The peculiarities of Miss Austen’s wit are so fine and delicate that they are, at all times, more easily felt than defined. To my apprehension, her humour bears a closer resemblance to that of Addison than to any other of the many modes of this British genius. The distinctions of time, subject, and literary custom are, of course, apparent, and the difference of sex may not account for much, for there was in “The Spectator” a distinctly feminine strain, and in Miss Austen’s character much that was masculine, though nothing of the masculine sort. Yet the likeness lies in the shared qualities of modesty, the minuteness of touch, and the avoidance of loud or glaring effects. There is in both authors a certain not unkind cruelty, a well-mannered and insatiable delight in the dissection of folly. Those who judge harshly often contrast Addison’s mildness with Swift’s savagery, or Miss Austen’s gentleness with Fielding’s boisterousness, yet in both writers may be found a restrained and ruthless pleasure in roasting a fool. A gentleman of the early eighteenth century might indulge this taste more freely than a lady of the nineteenth, and doubtless Miss Austen’s principles would have recoiled from such coarseness as the letter in “The Spectator” from a husband who, with all innocence and gusto, recounts his wife’s and friend’s trickery. Yet another “Spectator” letter, that of a young damsel wishing to marry Mr. Shapely and declaring, “He admires your _Spectators_ mightily,” might have been penned by a more refined Lydia Bennet of a bygone generation. Conversely, some (I think unjustly) have detected “cynicism” in Miss Austen’s satire of Mrs. Musgrove’s self-deceiving laments. Yet this term, so often misused, is applied not to rough invective but to gentle, oblique satire. If cynicism is the perception of the world’s duplicity, the awareness that motives are seldom pure, and that to seem is not to be, then every perceptive soul is a cynic. And in that sense, Miss Austen was assuredly one, finding in her own Mr. Bennet an epicurean delight in the dissection of fools and mean persons. I believe she did take such pleasure, and I hold her none the worse as a woman, though infinitely the better as an artist.

The greatness of Mr. Collins could not have been so perfectly portrayed had not Miss Austen so adroitly adjusted his figure to those of Mr. Bennet and Lady Catherine de Bourgh. The latter, like Mr. Collins himself, hath been accused of exaggeration, yet I find the charge, if valid, to be faint indeed. Even now, I doubt not that many a woman—though not of noble birth—might rival Lady Catherine in her overbearing manner and neglect of decorum. In the previous century, an earl’s daughter, long wedged in matrimonial authority, had ample occasion to cultivate such agreeable traits, which scarce present themselves in this age. As for Mr. Bennet, I am persuaded that Miss Austen, Miss Darcy, and even Miss Elizabeth herself were rather severe in their judgment of his “impropriety.” His wife, it is evident, was a woman of irredeemable folly; and unless he had chosen to shoot her or himself, there was no recourse for a man of sense but to adopt the stance of ironic detachment. From no other perspective is he culpable, save for an excusable and natural helplessness at the crisis of Lydia’s elopement. His sayings are the most acutely delicious in the conscious humorous vein—those that make us laugh with, not at—found in any of Miss Austen’s characters. It is hard to say whether he is most delightful when addressing his wife or when setting Mr. Collins to rights. The world, I believe, has rightly judged his consolation to his wife over the entail—“My dear, do not give way to such gloomy thoughts. Let us hope for better things. Let us flatter ourselves that _I_ may be the survivor”—and his inquiry to his cousin, “May I ask whether these pleasing attentions proceed from the impulse of the moment, or are the result of previous study?” to be among the finest of Miss Austen’s delightful surprises, those which thrill the reader as do the works of Swift, Fielding, or Thackeray, and none other.
