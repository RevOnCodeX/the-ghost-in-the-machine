In the present novel there appears that Swiftian quality which is found nowhere else in the character of the immortal, ineffable Mr. Collins. Mr. Collins is, indeed, a man of extraordinary magnitude; he is far greater than any figure that Addison ever delineated, and almost as great as the very spirits of Fielding or Swift themselves. It has been declared that no one could ever be like him, yet, in the first place, he bears an unmistakable resemblance to those masters, and remains alive and imperishable, more real than hundreds of prime ministers, archbishops, metals, semi‑metals, and distinguished philosophers. In the second place, it would be a rashness to assert that an actual Mr. Collins could not have existed at the close of the eighteenth century. It is most interesting that, within the same gallery, we possess what may be called a half‑finished study of him in the person of John Dashwood: the formality, the vulgarity, the meanness are there, but the portrait is only half alive and feels somewhat unnatural. Mr. Collins, however, is perfectly natural and wholly alive. For, though he is described as a “miniature,” there is something gigantic in the manner in which a certain, and indeed many, aspects of humanity—particularly the eighteenth‑century humanity of philistinism, of well‑meaning but inflexible morality, of formal pettiness, of grovelling respect for rank, of materialism, and of selfishness—are exhibited. I shall not maintain that any speech or action of this inestimable man is incapable of being reconciled with reality, nor would I be surprised if many of his words and deeds are historically true.

The greatness of Mr. Collins could not be so convincingly displayed had not his creator taken such artful care to interweave the figures of Mr. Bennet and Lady Catherine de Bourgh around him. Lady Catherine, like Mr. Collins herself, has been accused of exaggeration, yet the shade of colour that lends itself to that charge is exceedingly faint. Even now I do not think it impossible to discover persons—especially women not of noble birth—who are as overbearing, as self‑centred, as neglectful of good manners, as Lady Catherine. A century ago, an earl’s daughter, the Lady Powerful (if not exactly Bountiful) of a remote parish, rich and long since out of marital authority, could have cultivated these agreeable traits, which seldom present themselves in the present day. As for Mr. Bennet, Miss Austen, Mr. Darcy, and even Miss Elizabeth, were, I believe, rather severe upon him for the “impropriety” of his conduct. His wife was, without doubt, an irreclaimable fool; and unless he had taken his own life—or hers—there was no escape for a man of sense and spirit but the ironic. He is open to reproach only in an excusable, natural helplessness at the crisis of the elopement, and his utterances are the most acutely delightful in the consciously humorous vein—those passages which make us laugh with, not at, the speaker, a style which even Miss Austen herself has allowed to flow from the mouth of one of her characters. It is difficult to say whether he is most agreeable when conversing with his wife, or when putting Mr. Collins through his paces; yet the world has generally preferred to rank his consolation to the former, when she maunders over the entail, “My dear, do not give way to such gloomy thoughts. Let us hope for better things. Let us flatter ourselves that I may be the survivor,” and to his inquiry concerning the compliments which Mr. Collins has just reported as bestowed by himself upon Lady Catherine, “May I ask whether these pleasing attentions proceed from the impulse of the moment, or are the result of previous study?” These are the very touches which give Miss Austen’s readers the pleasant shocks, the delightful thrills, which are felt by the readers of Swift, of Fielding, and, we may add, of Thackeray, and which are felt by no other English novelist.
